Shipping the Vega-Lite Builder

After a few late nights, the Vega-Lite Builder is finally live in the Studio. The idea was simple: let people point, click and drag their way to a chart spec without hand-writing a single line of JSON.

It turns out "simple" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Encoding channels, scales, and the thousand little defaults that make a chart feel right — each one needed a sensible fallback so the preview never breaks while you tinker.

Why build another chart tool at all?

There are plenty of chart builders out there, and most of them are perfectly good. But almost all of them hide the grammar underneath. You drag a field onto "colour" and you get a chart, but you never learn why that choice worked, or how to undo it when it doesn't.

Vega-Lite is different because the grammar is the point. Every chart is a small, readable spec: here's the data, here's the mark, here's how fields map to position and colour and size. If you can read one spec, you can read them all. I wanted a builder that teaches that grammar instead of hiding it — click to build, then look at the spec it produced and actually recognise the pieces.

The hard part: sensible defaults

The genuinely difficult bit wasn't the UI. It was deciding what should happen when you haven't told it what to do yet. Pick a field for the x-axis but nothing for y? It needs to show something useful, not an error. Switch a mark from bar to line halfway through? The encodings that still make sense should survive; the ones that don't should quietly step aside.

Every one of those moments is a tiny design decision, and there are hundreds of them. Get them right and the tool feels like it's reading your mind. Get them wrong and it feels like fighting a form.

What's next

A few things are already on the list: saved specs you can come back to, a gallery of starting points, and a cleaner way to bring your own data in. But honestly the roadmap is going to be shaped more by what people actually trip over than by my own guesses.

So if you give it a spin and something feels off, I genuinely want to hear about it. The whole point is to lower the barrier between "I have some data" and "I have a chart I'm proud of."

Gradient fills in Deneb

Someone asked this week whether you can do proper gradient fills in Deneb. Short answer: yes, and they look lovely when used with restraint.

The trick is a linear gradient definition in the mark's fill, with stops tied to a field or just a fixed pair of colours. I'll usually keep it to a single hue stepping from light to dark — a rainbow gradient on a bar chart is a fast way to make a beautiful chart that says nothing.

A quick rule of thumb

If the gradient encodes a value, make sure the legend explains it. If it's purely decorative, keep it subtle enough that nobody mistakes it for data.

An xG table for FPL

Gameweek after gameweek I keep coming back to the same question: is this striker actually good, or just lucky? Expected goals (xG) is the cleanest way I've found to tell the difference, so I built a little table that lines up each player's goals against their xG.

The players sitting well above their xG line are the ones to watch for a regression — the fairy tale usually ends. The ones below it, quietly racking up chances without the end product, are often the better transfer in.

It's not gospel. But over a season it's been a far better guide than the back pages.

Mapping my kayak spots

A slightly less data-heavy one. I've started pinning every decent kayak launch I find to a map on this site — partly so I stop forgetting the good ones, partly as an excuse to play with mapping in the browser.

Turns out a surprising amount of the fun is in the little details: a sensible zoom, markers that don't overlap into mush, and a popup that tells you what you actually need to know before you drive an hour with a boat on the roof.
